\title{Smaller looks rounder: correcting resolution-dependent bias of shape descriptors in bioimage and radiomics software}
\name{Anonymous Author(s)}
\address{Affiliation}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Circularity and sphericity are ubiquitous in cell morphometry and radiomics, but the perimeter and surface estimates behind them depend on resolution. The default estimators of scikit-image (hence CellProfiler), OpenCV and ImageJ make the same object rounder the fewer pixels it covers, which we explain with a closed-form model; IBSI sphericity of a sphere drops from $0.92$ to $0.82$ as CT slices thicken from 1 to 5\,mm. Even Crofton estimators fail for small objects and thick slices, as they miss chords shorter than the sampling step. We show that the missed chords number one sixth of the single-sample runs, yielding a parameter-free run-corrected Crofton estimator for 2D and anisotropic 3D grids. Averaged over shapes it is unbiased to within $0.005$ down to 2-pixel radii, keeps smooth lesions' sphericity bias within $0.005$ up to 5\,mm slices, and is five times more accurate than PyRadiomics on lung-tumour phantoms. On re-segmented nuclei, the default estimator shifts the form factor by up to $3.3$ standard deviations and reverses a biological contrast at $10\times$; corrected estimators do not.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
Morphometry, radiomics, harmonization, digital geometry, stereology
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Shape descriptors of segmented objects are a staple of quantitative imaging. Nuclear circularity quantifies pleomorphism in histopathology, CellProfiler's \emph{FormFactor} is part of every Cell Painting profile~\cite{bray2016,stirling2021}, and sphericity is a standardized radiomics feature of the Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative (IBSI)~\cite{zwanenburg2020}. Both are ratios of a size to a boundary measure, $\ff=4\pi A/P^2$ and $\Psi=(36\pi V^2)^{1/3}/S$, equal to $1$ for a disk and a ball. Studies routinely pool data of different resolution: slides scanned at $20\times$ and $40\times$, cell types of different sizes, CT series with 1--5\,mm slices.

The area or volume of a mask is easy to estimate, since the pixel count is unbiased under random placement; the boundary is not~\cite{kulpa1977,lindblad2005}. Kulpa~\cite{kulpa1977} derived the large-object length factor of chain codes; Crofton estimators, which count boundary crossings of lattice lines, are unbiased in the large-object limit~\cite{legland2007}, and a recent comparison recommends them for 2D perimeters~\cite{legland2026}; MATLAB patched its circularity for small objects in 2023~\cite{matlab2023}. Three gaps remain: (a) no model explains the \emph{size dependence} of the estimators shipped in open-source tools, which is what breaks comparisons across resolutions; (b) Crofton estimators are themselves biased when objects are small or slices thick, because chords shorter than the sampling step are never seen, and no correction exists; (c) the consequences in real pipelines are unquantified.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A \emph{run-corrected Crofton} (RCC) estimator: we show that, for smooth boundaries, the expected number of missed chords equals one sixth of the number of single-sample runs, which removes the small-object and thick-slice bias without parameters, in 2D and on anisotropic 3D grids. (ii) A closed-form model, $P\approx\kappa(L-2\pi\delta)$, of the default estimators' size dependence, and a post-hoc correction (F1) for existing feature tables. (iii) An audit of eight implementations against ground truth; evidence on 89k real nuclei (shifted features, distorted biological contrasts) and on 351 real CT reconstructions, where IBSI sphericity looks thickness-robust only through cancelling errors.

\section{Why default estimators depend on size}
\label{sec:model}
Let $X\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ have a smooth boundary of length $L$, digitized on a unit grid as $D=X\cap\mathbb{Z}^2$ with random sub-pixel position and orientation. Most tools trace a polygon along $D$ whose edges take a few lattice directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}a). If a boundary element $ds$ at tangent angle $\theta$ becomes polygon length $g(\theta)\,ds$, and the polygon lies on average a distance $\delta$ inside $\partial X$, Steiner's formula (a closed curve moved inward by $\delta$ has length $L-2\pi\delta$) gives
\begin{equation}
P \approx \kappa\,(L-2\pi\delta),\qquad \kappa=\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\textstyle\int_0^{2\pi} g(\theta)\,d\theta .
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
\emph{Chain codes}, used by scikit-image's default \texttt{regionprops} perimeter (hence CellProfiler), OpenCV's \texttt{arcLength} and MATLAB up to R2022b, join the centres of boundary pixels by steps of length $1$ or $\sqrt2$: $g(\theta)=|\cos\theta|+(\sqrt2-1)|\sin\theta|$ for $|\theta|\le\pi/4$, so $\kappa=8(\sqrt2-1)/\pi\approx1.055$~\cite{kulpa1977}. Along the grid axis closest to the normal, the last pixel centre inside $X$ lies at a uniform distance in $[0,1)$ from $\partial X$, i.e.\ $\cos\theta/2$ on average perpendicular to it, so $\delta=\tfrac{4}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\tfrac{\cos\theta}{2}d\theta=\sqrt2/\pi\approx0.45$. For a disk of radius $r$ pixels,
\begin{equation}
\ff_{\text{chain}}(r)\approx \kappa^{-2}\,\big(r/(r-\sqrt2/\pi)\big)^{2},
\label{eq:disk}
\end{equation}
which exceeds $1$ for $r<8.7$\,px and tends to $0.899$. \emph{Marching squares} (PyRadiomics; IBSI meshes) have the same $\kappa$ but $\delta=0$, a constant factor $\kappa^{-2}$ on $\ff$; \emph{pixel-edge polygons} have $\kappa=4/\pi$, $\delta=0$; ImageJ cuts the corners of pixel-edge outlines, a hybrid; MATLAB R2023a corrects $\delta$ but not $\kappa$~\cite{matlab2023}. In 3D, marching cubes on binary masks overestimates the surface increasingly with the slice-to-pixel ratio (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}e).
\emph{F1 (post hoc).} Inverting Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}, $\hat P=P_{\text{chain}}/\kappa+2\pi\delta'$ repairs an existing scikit-image/CellProfiler perimeter column without the masks. Small objects (2--3\,px) lose extra length, so we calibrate $\delta'$ once on the synthetic shapes (minimax $\ff$ error, giving $0.52$) and use $\delta'=\tfrac12$, i.e.\ $\hat P=P/1.055+\pi$; no real data are used.

\section{Run-corrected Crofton estimator}
\label{sec:rcc}
\noindent\textbf{Crofton estimator.} Consider lattice directions $d$ (4 in 2D, 13 in 3D, in physical units for voxel spacing $s$, volume $v$). Lines along $d$ through the pixel or voxel centres have density $\lambda_d=\lVert d\rVert/v$ per unit length or area orthogonal to $d$, and each line is sampled every $h_d=\lVert d\rVert$. If $N_d$ is the number of runs (maximal sequences of object samples) along such lines, then $N_d/\lambda_d$ estimates the object's projection orthogonal to $d$ (half the crossing measure for non-convex objects), and the Cauchy--Crofton formulas give
\begin{equation}
\hat P=\pi\textstyle\sum_d w_d N_d/\lambda_d \;(2\text{D}),\qquad \hat S=4\sum_d w_d N_d/\lambda_d \;(3\text{D}),
\label{eq:crofton}
\end{equation}
with $w_d$ the fraction of directions (circle or sphere) closest to $\pm d$, computed for the actual spacing~\cite{legland2007,legland2016}.

\noindent\textbf{Missed chords.} A run is seen only if its chord contains a sample. With a uniformly random sample offset, a chord of length $\ell$ yields $k$ samples with $P(k{=}0)=\max(0,1-\ell/h)$, and $P(k{=}1)=\ell/h$ for $\ell\le h$, $2-\ell/h$ for $h<\ell\le2h$. Short chords occur where a line grazes the boundary: at distance $t$ from the tangent line of a boundary with curvature radius $\rho$, $\ell\approx2\sqrt{2\rho t}$, and since $t$ is uniform, the chord-length density is linear near zero, $f(\ell)\approx a\ell$. Hence the expected numbers of missed chords and of single-sample runs are
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[\text{missed}_d]&=\textstyle\int_0^{h}a\ell\,(1-\tfrac{\ell}{h})\,d\ell=\tfrac{ah^2}{6},\nonumber\\
\mathbb{E}[n_{1,d}]&=\textstyle\int_0^{h}a\ell\,\tfrac{\ell}{h}\,d\ell+\int_h^{2h}a\ell\,(2-\tfrac{\ell}{h})\,d\ell=ah^2,
\end{align}
so, whatever $a$ and $h$, the missed chords are one sixth of the single-sample runs:
\begin{equation}
N_d^{\text{RCC}}=N_d+\tfrac16\,n_{1,d},
\label{eq:rcc}
\end{equation}
where $n_{1,d}$ counts runs of exactly one sample along $d$. Plugging $N_d^{\text{RCC}}$ into Eq.~\eqref{eq:crofton} gives the RCC estimator. It needs no parameter, no resampling and one extra comparison per direction; on anisotropic grids $h_d$ differs between directions, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:rcc} handles automatically.
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}
\noindent\textbf{Synthetic ground truth.} 5{,}000 shapes of known perimeter and area: ellipses (axis ratios $1, 0.6, 0.35$) and two families of random smooth star-shaped ``nuclei'', equivalent radii 2--48\,px, random rotation and sub-pixel offset. In 3D, randomly rotated spheres and $1{:}0.75{:}0.5$ ellipsoids (equivalent radius 3--20\,mm) on $1\times1\times s$\,mm grids, $s\in[1,5]$. We run the libraries' own code (scikit-image 0.26~\cite{vanderwalt2014}, OpenCV 4.13, PyRadiomics 3.0.1~\cite{vangriethuysen2017}); CellProfiler's FormFactor uses scikit-image's perimeter (checked in its source); ImageJ's~\cite{schindelin2012} traced perimeter is ported, MATLAB R2023a's formula~\cite{matlab2023} implemented.

\noindent\textbf{Real nuclei.} PanNuke fold 1~\cite{gamper2019} (H\&E, $40\times$, annotated types; 43{,}519 nuclei) and BBBC038~\cite{caicedo2019} (22{,}858 nuclei). Lower magnification is obtained by \emph{re-digitizing} each annotated mask on a $2\times$ or $4\times$ coarser grid (block mean ${\ge}0.5$), and, realistically, by \emph{re-segmenting} block-averaged images independently at each resolution with the pretrained Cellpose~\cite{stringer2021} \texttt{nuclei} model, matching nuclei by IoU${}\ge0.5$ (12{,}279 PanNuke and 10{,}454 BBBC038 nuclei). We report the paired shift $\Delta/\sigma$ (mean change in $\ff$ over its native s.d.; s.e.\ ${\approx}0.01$), Spearman's $\rho_A$ between area and $\ff$, Cohen's $d$ between PanNuke nucleus types, and the $10\times$ balanced-accuracy drop of a $40\times$-trained logistic regression on shape features (5 image-level splits).

\noindent\textbf{Lung tumours.} The 63 CTs of the Medical Segmentation Decathlon lung set~\cite{antonelli2022} provide real tumour shapes; each tumour (${\ge}300$\,mm$^3$; 64 tumours, radius 3--45\,mm) is turned into a phantom: the mask is resampled to 0.5\,mm and smoothed ($\sigma=1.5$\,mm), its $0.5$ level set defines a surface of known sphericity (0.60--0.96, from a fine mesh), point-sampled at 0.7\,mm in-plane with 0.7--5\,mm slices. Real reconstructions come from RIDER Lung CT~\cite{zhao2024rider}: 32 patients scanned twice on the same day, each scan reconstructed at 1.25, 2.5 and 5\,mm with a sharp and a smooth kernel, with radiologist tumour segmentations for every reconstruction (351 series with uniform slice spacing).

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\noindent\textbf{Synthetic 2D (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}b).} The chain-code tools overestimate circularity by $+0.55$ at $r=2$\,px and $+0.11$ at $r=4$\,px and underestimate it by $0.08$ at $r=48$, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:disk} predicts (dashed). ImageJ follows the same trend ($+0.09$ to $-0.08$); PyRadiomics and MATLAB R2023a are offset by $\kappa^{-2}$ ($-0.08$ to $-0.09$). Crofton is unbiased for large objects but overestimates circularity by $0.050$ at $r=2$ and $0.013$ at $r=4$\,px. RCC reduces this to ${\le}0.005$ at every size, at similar variance; for the most irregular shapes it halves the bias at $r=2$ ($0.10\to0.05$).

\noindent\textbf{Synthetic 3D (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}e).} PyRadiomics sphericity is biased by $-0.08$ at 1\,mm and $-0.17$ at 5\,mm slices for every lesion size; isotropic resampling, as IBSI recommends, \emph{increases} the bias to $-0.19$. RCC stays within $\pm0.005$ for all $R\ge5$\,mm and thicknesses (Crofton: up to $+0.046$). On the 64 (irregular) lung-tumour phantoms, PyRadiomics is biased by $-0.065$ at 0.7\,mm and $-0.136$ at 5\,mm slices, Crofton by $+0.005$ and $+0.042$, and RCC by $+0.002$ and $+0.026$ (10--90\%: $[0.004,0.050]$), a fivefold smaller error than PyRadiomics; the residual comes from tumour poles thinner than a slice and from boundary irregularity.

\noindent\textbf{Real reconstructions (RIDER).} Test--retest repeatability is the same for all estimators (ICC 0.81--0.94 per reconstruction), so RCC costs no precision. From 1.25 to 5\,mm slices, the same tumours' RCC sphericity rises by $+0.097$ (sharp kernel) and $+0.098$ (smooth; both $p<10^{-8}$, Wilcoxon, $n=55$--58), because thick-slice images and their contours are smoother: the measured object changes. PyRadiomics hides this ($+0.009$, $+0.010$, n.s.) only because its own bias falls with thickness: the PyRadiomics--RCC gap changes by $-0.088$, matching the $-0.078$ predicted by the phantoms. An apparently ``robust'' IBSI sphericity thus results from two cancelling errors, not from invariance.

\noindent\textbf{Real nuclei (Fig.~\ref{fig:main}c,d; Table~\ref{tab:real}).} With the default estimator, halving the resolution shifts the mean $\ff$ by $0.7$--$0.95\sigma$ and quartering it by $2.5$--$5.6\sigma$ in both settings and modalities; at $\tfrac14$ resolution the median BBBC038 nucleus has an impossible $\ff>1$. The default also creates an area--roundness correlation ($\rho_A=-0.34$, $-0.56$). Crofton reduces the shifts to $0.06$--$0.51\sigma$ and RCC to $0.03$--$0.28\sigma$; F1, whose calibrated offset may also absorb part of the smoothing of low-resolution masks, is the most stable under re-segmentation (${\le}0.05\sigma$). Residual shifts are partly genuine: coarse grids remove real boundary detail.

\noindent\textbf{Biological contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:bio}).} Inflammatory nuclei are smaller than neoplastic ones (median radius 9.5 vs.\ 13.9\,px at $40\times$), so the default inflates their extra roundness from $d=0.35$--$0.40$ to $0.67$. At $10\times$, the default reverses the sign of the connective--neoplastic contrast ($-0.62\to+0.11$), whereas RCC preserves it ($-0.77\to-0.72$). When scanners are mixed (inflammatory at $20\times$, neoplastic at $40\times$), the default reports $d=1.62$ instead of $0.35$--$0.49$. A $40\times$-trained type classifier loses $0.115$ balanced accuracy at $10\times$ with the default $\ff$ and $0.031$ with RCC.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}
The default circularity of scikit-image, CellProfiler and OpenCV distorts any comparison across pixel sizes (Eq.~\eqref{eq:disk}); constant-offset estimators mislead absolute thresholds and, in 3D, can mask real protocol effects. We recommend RCC for new analyses on the native grid, F1 to repair existing CellProfiler/scikit-image tables, and reporting object sizes in pixels. Limitations: Eq.~\eqref{eq:rcc} assumes smooth boundaries (objects of about one slice remain biased), and block-averaging only approximates re-imaging.
